Demonstrations of netstacklat, the Linux BPF CO-RE version.

netstacklat monitors how long network packets (SKBs) have spent within
the Linux kernel's ingress network stack at various points in the
stack and reports the overall latency distribution in histograms.

The above output shows how the latency varies at each of netstacklat's
probe points. Here we can note that the latency distribution at
ip-start (which is netstacklat's earliest probe, running at the start
of IP-layer processing before e.g. netfilter) has a bimodal
distribution with peaks in the 65-128ns and 1.02-2.05us bins. This
bimodal distribution remains at later TCP probe points.

We can also see that the tcp-socket-read latency (which shows the
total time until the SKB payload is actually read by a user space
application) has a trimodal distribution and a small number of reads
that even exceed one second. In contrast, all UDP probe points have
relatively narrow distributions, only reaching a handful of
microseconds.


We can use -e/--enable-probes to limit the monitoring to the probe
points we're interested in. Furthermore, with -I/--groupby-interface
and -C/--groupby-cgroup we can group the data per interface and cgroup
(note that all cgroup options only apply to {tcp,udp}-socket-read as
netstacklat is unable to determine the cgroup at earlier probes).

Now we can see that the two peaks in the previous bimodal distribution
at ip-start come from different interfaces, nanosecond delays on
ens3f0 and microsecond delays on ens3f1. In this case, the higher
delays on ens3f1 are caused by tc-BPF programs attached to the ens3f1,
processing and delaying all packets on that interface.

The tcp-socket-read latency has a different split. We can now see that
the first peak in the previous trimodal distribution come from an
iperf3 cgroup (systemd service). The two higher peaks as well as the
multi-second delays are instead related to the nginx service. Note
that all nginx traffic comes from the ens3f0 interface, so the delays
here are not caused by the tc-BPF processing seen at the ip-start
probe.

To further limit our monitoring, we can use -i/--interfaces and
-c/--cgroups to apply in-kernel filtering on the interfaces and
cgroups of interest. By using -r/--report-interval we can get periodic
reports.

In the output above, we can see that the slower reads (above 33ms)
only occur during certain periods. In this case it is caused by
periodic traffic spikes that temporarily overloads the nginx server.


One aspect that can cause high SKB latency for tcp-socket-read
specifically, is TCP head of line (HoL) blocking, where the TCP stack
holds on to segments that arrived out of order until all preceding
segments have arrived. As delays due to HoL blocking is primarily
related to factors outside of the local host (the loss and latency on
the network path the traffic is sent over), netstacklat by default
filters out measurements from any SKBs that may potentially be HoL
blocked. However, in some cases it may also be interesting to include
delays from HoL blocked segments, so the HoL filtering can be disabled
with -y/--include-hol-delay option.

The output above comes from a different scenario where iperf3 traffic
is received over a link with 1% packet loss and a 30ms RTT. As can be
seen, a few SKBs sit around in the local network stack for 10s of
milliseconds (with a small peak around the 30ms RTT) before they are
read due to the TCP stack having to wait on lost segments to be
retransmitted (in a couple of instances multiple times, causing delays
greater than the RTT). Without --include-hol-delay (i.e. excluding
measurements from HoL delayed SKBs), the latency stays below 1ms here.
